RQ2: Evolution of performance and memory retrieval across dependent subtasks. Figure R2a shows mean judge score at each subtask position, pooled over the three backbones (n = 360 observations per system per position). At the first sub-question (q0), where the memory store is still empty, all four configurations perform similarly (0.38–0.44) and the matched memory–stateless gap is near zero (−0.03 to +0.03), as expected. From q1 onward - the positions that depend on information established earlier in the task - memory-enabled agents outperform the stateless baseline (Figure R2b; gaps matched per model × task × position): mem0 sustains a gap of +0.05 to +0.10 that persists to the final position (+0.098 at q5), hindsight peaks at +0.14 (q1 and q3) but declines to +0.03 by q5, while acontext stays close to the baseline throughout (−0.03 to +0.06). Scores decline with position for all configurations (OLS slopes −0.011 to −0.027 per position), reflecting the increasing dependency depth of later sub-questions; the stateless baseline shows the sharpest first-step drop (0.41 at q0 to 0.15 at q1, the first position requiring information from a previous step), whereas mem0 has the shallowest decline (−0.011 per position, p = 0.056) and the highest late-task performance (0.320 at q4–q5 vs. 0.243 for stateless, +32% relative). Retrieval activity mirrors memory accumulation: retrieved records rise from 0 at q0 toward the top-k = 3 cap (hindsight reaches 3.0 by q3; mem0 2.84 at q5), with a memory write after every subtask; acontext's skill-listing retrieval is roughly constant (2.0–2.6 skills) across positions. Together, these patterns show that the benefit of memory materialises precisely at sub-questions that depend on earlier decisions - evidence of within-task memory reuse, not cross-task transfer.
